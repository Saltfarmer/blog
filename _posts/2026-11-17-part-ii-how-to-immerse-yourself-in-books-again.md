# **Why did we lose the capacity to get lost in books—and how can we rebuild deep focus and rediscover the joy of reading?**

---

**Sven Birkerts**, a literary critic, wrote what is probably one of the earliest accounts of deep reading as a distinct, describable state of consciousness, years before “online addiction” was a recognized concern.

He used the term “deep reading” for what he called *“the slow and meditative possession of a book,”* writing that *“we don’t just read the words, we dream our lives in their vicinity,”* and describing how, once inside a demanding book, *“the energies that otherwise tend to stream outward through a thousand channels of distraction are marshaled by the cadences of the prose.”*

In this second part, I’ll share strategies to use *while* reading, explain how to approach deep reading as a long-term skill-building process, and explore some common pitfalls to watch for along the way.

## Staying Focused

![]({{site.baseurl}}/assets/images/pasted-1794924518684.png)

Even after you’ve started reading, it’s normal for your mind to wander, for the book to suddenly feel harder, or for a sentence to trip you up. This part is all about how to handle those moments. The idea is to give yourself lots of little successes as you go.

**Notice when your mind starts to wander and gently bring your focus back.** When you notice your mind wandering while you read, just gently bring your focus back to the page. It’s normal for your eyes to keep moving even when your mind isn’t paying attention. If you realize you’ve zoned out, just go back to the last spot you remember and start again. Researchers have found that when people read like this, their eyes move differently. They spend longer looking at words and don’t go back to reread tricky parts, but they aren’t really taking in the meaning. Most of the time, people don’t even notice it’s happening. The key is to catch yourself as soon as you notice your mind has drifted and calmly refocus, rather than forcing yourself to concentrate the whole time. Schooler and colleagues (2004) tracked readers’ eyes and caught “mindless reading,”[^18] where the eyes travel across the lines while attention is elsewhere. During these periods, fixations[^19] grow longer and readers make fewer of the corrective backward eye movements that normally catch confusing text. (Schooler et al., 2004; Reichle et al., 2010)

![]({{site.baseurl}}/assets/images/pasted-1794924559987.png)

> **Figure 5.** The image shows a map of where a reader’s eyes stopped and moved while reading. This kind of eye-tracking data helped researchers discover that people can zone out while their eyes keep moving across the page.[^20]

**Reread the confusing sentence.** If a sentence doesn’t make sense, slow down and read it again. This helps you understand what you’re reading. Good readers often reread tough sentences or paragraphs, and that’s completely normal. Trying to avoid rereading, like some speed-reading methods suggest, usually makes it harder to really get the meaning. It’s better to reread until you understand, instead of forcing yourself to move on and getting more confused later. Skilled readers do this all the time, especially with difficult books. *Why It Works:* Rayner[^21] and his team looked at lots of reading studies and found that understanding what you read depends on how well you recognize words and make sense of language, not on how fast your eyes move. (Rayner et al., 2016)

**Surrender to the story, stop monitoring yourself.** Let yourself get lost in the story instead of worrying about staying focused. When you really get into a book, you stop overthinking and just enjoy it. If you keep checking whether you’re paying attention or judging the story, it actually makes it harder to get absorbed. So if you notice yourself doing that, just let it go and keep reading. *Why It Works:* Green and Brock (2000) found that when you get really caught up in a story, you stop picking it apart, you don’t notice as many mistakes or problems because you’re focused on following the plot instead of judging the writing. (Green & Brock, 2000; Mrazek et al., 2013)

**Practice focusing your attention outside of reading.** Try practicing your focus outside of reading. For two weeks, spend ten minutes a day just paying attention to your breathing. When your mind wanders, gently bring it back to your breath. This simple exercise can help you get better at refocusing when you’re reading and your mind drifts. *Why it works?* Mrazek and his team (2013) did a study where one group practiced mindfulness like this for two weeks, while another group took a nutrition class. The mindfulness group raised their GRE reading scores by about 16 percentile points on average and had fewer moments where their minds wandered. The biggest improvements were in people who were usually the most easily distracted. Just keep in mind, their test used short GRE reading passages right after the training, not long, tough books read for fun. So, you might not see the same results, but the basic idea (training your attention) should still help. (Mrazek et al., 2013)[^22]

## The Long Way Back

To rebuild your deep reading skills, we are going to focus on long-term progress, this is not a single session thing. One good night might just mean the book was easy or you were relaxed. One tough night could be a hard chapter or a bad mood. So, there are going to be ups and downs. Real improvement takes time, so don’t judge yourself too quickly.

**Build your reading stamina slowly.** Start with short reading sessions and make them a little longer each week. Take breaks in between instead of trying to read for hours all at once. To try this approach, pick a reading time you can handle, like fifteen minutes, and do that every night for a week. The next week, add five minutes. Make sure you really rest between sessions. *Why It Works:* Ericsson[^23] and colleagues (1993) found that expert musicians improved by practicing in short, focused sessions with recovery time between them, and reading instruction for children often starts at ten to twenty minutes and increases over months. (Ericsson et al., 1993)

> **The two-week rebuild: a graded nightly ramp**
> Short sessions at the same time each night, lengthened week by week. Wolf and Harris rebuilt in about two weeks, by their own report.
>
> - **WEEK 1 — 15 min:** Same time, same chair, every night. Warm-start by rereading the last page.
> - **WEEK 2 — 20 min (+5 on week 1):** Add five minutes. Disconnect fully between sessions.
> - **ABOUT 2 WEEKS:** Wolf and Harris both reported rebuilding within about two weeks, self-reported.
>
> *Judge by weekly cadence, not any single night. Track the books you finish, not the sessions.*

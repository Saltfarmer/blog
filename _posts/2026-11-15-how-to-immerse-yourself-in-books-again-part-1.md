Hello everyone! Tonight in my free time, I want to share something a bit different from my usual data stuff.

Not gonna lie, lately I've been thinking about how hard it is to just sit down and read a book. Remember back in the day when you could open a novel or a long article and just disappear into it for hours? You'd picture the scenes in your head and actually feel what the characters were going through.

Now? I read two pages, get fidgety, and suddenly my hand is reaching for my phone to check notifications or scroll through random feeds. I'm not sure if anyone else feels this way, tho I suspect most of us have traded deep focus for cheap, quick dopamine snacks.

Even a tech writer named **Nicholas Carr** ran into this exact wall back in 2007. He realized he couldn't concentrate on a book for more than a page or two anymore. Instead of just complaining about it, he went full extreme: he moved to a house in the mountains with zero cell signal, canceled his Twitter, paused Facebook, and shut down his blog just so his brain could calm down enough to write a book.

> **The good news:** You didn't actually lose your ability to get lost in a book. Your brain just got rewired to expect constant notifications. Deep focus is a muscle—if you ignore it, it gets weak, and if you train it again, it comes back.

You don't need to move to a mountain cabin to fix this. In this first part, we are going to focus on **setting up your room** so you don't have to rely on willpower.

## 1. Put Your Phone in Another Room

![]({{site.baseurl}}/assets/images/pasted-1794760058912.png)

Before you even open a book, take your phone and leave it in a completely different room.

**Do not just put it face-down** next to you on the sofa or on silent mode on your desk. That doesn't work. A 2017 study on "brain drain" tested people doing focus-heavy tasks and found that the mere presence of their own phone sitting on the desk made them perform worse—even when they never touched it or looked at it.

- **Why this happens:** Part of your brain is actively spending energy just trying *not* to check the phone.
- **What to do:** Plug your phone into a charger in another room. If you need a timer or an alarm, use a cheap digital watch or a physical clock instead. Distance is what matters here.

## 2. Cut the Internet at the Source

![]({{site.baseurl}}/assets/images/pasted-1794760085242.png)

Trying to fight the urge to check social media while reading is exhausting. Instead of relying on willpower, just remove the option completely.

Look at how people like **Nicholas Carr** or writer **Hugh McGuire** got their reading habits back: they didn't just "try harder" to focus—they physically blocked the distractions. McGuire simply banned all devices from his bedroom and took scheduled breaks from social media.

- **Extra Example:** Think of it like trying to diet while keeping a box of donuts right next to your keyboard. You're going to lose that battle eventually. If the donuts aren't in the room, you don't have to think about them.
- **What to do:** Turn off your phone's Wi-Fi/data, set your home router on a night timer, or use a website/app blocker before your reading session. Make it annoying to go online so reading becomes the easiest thing to do.

## Conclusion: Fix the Room First

Getting your reading groove back isn't about forcing yourself to stare at pages when your brain is screaming for a dopamine hit. Start by making distractions physically hard to reach: **toss your phone into another room and kill your internet connection before you open page one.**

That's all for Part 1, folks! In the next post, we'll look at how to actually train your focus stamina step-by-step once the distractions are gone. Thanks for reading, and I'll catch you in the next post.

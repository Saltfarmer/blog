Hello everyone! Tonight in my free time, I want to write down some notes on productivity—mostly because I need to record a lot of stuff so I can remember it properly, and also because my to-do list has been getting a bit out of control lately.

If you have ever fallen down the productivity rabbit hole, you have definitely heard of **Getting Things Done (GTD)** by David Allen. Its core rule is famous for a reason:

> "Your mind is for having ideas, not holding them."
>
> – David Allen

By capturing every open loop—every bug to fix, email to reply to, or random script idea—into external lists organized by context (`@Computer`, `@Office`, `@Calls`), GTD eliminates that nagging background anxiety of forgetting something important.

Not gonna lie, the capture habit is awesome. Yet for engineers, data analysts, and researchers, pure GTD often hits a brick wall. **Cal Newport**—author of *Deep Work* and *Slow Productivity*—has spent years dissecting why classic GTD struggles in modern knowledge work, and how we can combine GTD's capture engine with stricter execution controls.

## 1. The Flaw in Pure GTD: The Infinite To-Do Queue

![]({{site.baseurl}}/assets/images/pasted-1795007881158.png)

Here is the catch: GTD is a **capture and organization system**, not a **capacity or attention management system**.

When you capture every Whatsapp message, ad-hoc data request, and side-project idea into a clean GTD list, two problems sneak up on you:

1. **Unbounded Work-in-Progress (WIP):** GTD makes it so frictionless to say "yes" to small tasks because your system handles storing them. Over time, your `Next Actions` list balloons into hundreds of minor items.
2. **Fragmented Attention:** GTD encourages checking your context lists whenever you have a spare 15 minutes. For analytical or coding work, jumping between small tasks destroys cognitive momentum and leaves zero uninterrupted blocks for **Deep Work**.

**Extra Example:** Imagine trying to build an end-to-end machine learning pipeline, but every 15 minutes you stop to answer an email, rename a column in an old spreadsheet, and check a Jira ticket. By 5 PM, you are completely exhausted, you checked off 12 tiny items, and your actual model hasn't moved an inch.

## 2. Cal Newport’s Hybrid Architecture

Rather than throwing GTD in the trash, Newport keeps GTD's capture discipline and wraps it inside three stricter constraints:

### A. Multi-Scale Planning (Quarter → Week → Day)

![]({{site.baseurl}}/assets/images/pasted-1795007957758.png)

Instead of working bottom-up from an endless list of tasks, you plan top-down:

- **Quarterly:** Define 2–3 major objectives that actually move the needle.
- **Weekly:** Review your GTD lists once a week, pick the specific tasks required for your quarterly goals, and ruthlessly defer or delete the rest.
- **Daily:** Build a concrete **Time-Block Plan** before the workday starts.

### B. Time-Blocking Over List-Picking

In pure GTD, you stare at a context list and ask, *"What should I do right now?"* Every time you finish a task, you burn willpower deciding what to pick next.

In Newport's system, **every hour of your workday is assigned a specific job in advance**:

- **Deep Work Blocks (90–180 mins):** Reserved for hard cognitive tasks (writing code, designing data schemas, debugging complex logic) with zero chat or email notifications.
- **Shallow Batch Blocks (30–60 mins):** Dedicated windows to blitz through standard GTD `@Email`, `@Admin`, and `@Calls` tasks all at once.

### C. Strict WIP Limits ("Pull" vs. "Push")

Borrowing from Lean manufacturing, Newport advises separating your GTD projects into two buckets:

- **Active Queue (Strictly Capped at 3–5 items):** The *only* projects you are allowed to schedule onto your daily time blocks.
- **Holding Tank (Unlimited):** Everything else you captured via GTD. New tasks cannot enter the Active Queue until an existing project is finished.

## 3. Other Popular Frameworks That Back This Up

What I find interesting is that Cal Newport isn't alone here. Several other well-known productivity thinkers arrived at the exact same conclusion—that capturing everything without strict limits leads to burnout:

- **Oliver Burkeman (*Four Thousand Weeks*) & The "Open/Closed" List:** Burkeman points out that GTD's promise of "clearing the decks" is a trap—the faster you clear small tasks, the more people hand you new ones. His fix mirrors Newport's WIP limit: keep two lists—an unlimited **Open List** (your GTD Holding Tank) and a **Closed List** capped at **10 items max**. You cannot move a new task onto your Closed List until you finish or drop one.
- **Personal Kanban (Jim Benson):** Originating from Toyota's manufacturing floor, Kanban's number one rule is **"Stop starting, start finishing."** Visualizing your tasks in `Backlog` → `Doing (Max 3)` → `Done` forces you to confront your real capacity instead of pretending you can juggle 20 active projects at once.
- **Tiago Forte’s PARA Method (*Building a Second Brain*):** Forte actually worked closely with GTD principles before creating PARA (Projects, Areas, Resources, Archives). His key upgrade to GTD is separating **actionable projects** (which have a clear deadline and outcome) from **reference knowledge**, so your task manager doesn't turn into a cluttered graveyard of "someday maybe" articles and notes.

## 4. Summary Workflow: Putting It All Together

If you want to run this hybrid setup starting tomorrow, the daily loop looks like this:

1. **Capture (GTD):** Dump every commitment, bug, and random idea into an inbox immediately so your brain stays clear.
2. **Clarify & Cap (GTD + WIP Limit / Burkeman's Closed List):** Delete non-essential tasks. Put top priorities into your capped **Active Queue (3–5 projects)**; park everything else in a **Holding Tank**.
3. **Time-Block (Newport):** Assign your Active Queue tasks to specific hours on your calendar—protecting morning blocks for deep analytical work and batching shallow GTD tasks into one afternoon block.
4. **Shutdown Ritual (Newport):** At the end of the day, sweep any loose ends back into your GTD inbox, sketch tomorrow's time blocks, and completely disconnect.

## Conclusion: Stop Managing Lists, Start Protecting Hours

GTD is still one of the best capture habits ever invented, so don't stop writing things down. Just remember that a well-organized list of 200 tasks is still 200 tasks you don't have time to do.

**Actionable takeaway:** Tonight, look at your current to-do list, pick **at most 3 active projects** for this week, hide the rest in a "Holding Tank" folder, and block out your first 90-minute Deep Work session on tomorrow's calendar before you go to sleep.

> "What we choose to focus on and what we choose to ignore—plays in defining the quality of our life."
>
> – Cal Newport
